At the start line of a big city marathon this fall, the loudest cheer did not go to the elite field. It went to a pacing group holding a hand-painted sign that read "First timers over 45." Roughly two hundred runners gathered behind it, stretching calves, adjusting watches, and taking selfies with people they had met twenty minutes earlier.

Race directors have noticed the same shift across the calendar. The fastest-growing slice of the marathon field is no longer twenty-somethings chasing a personal best. It is people in their forties and fifties, many of whom did not run a step until a few years ago.

## A midlife project with a finish line

The reasons runners give are remarkably consistent. Children have grown old enough to need less supervision. A doctor has mentioned blood pressure. A friend ran one and would not stop talking about it. A marathon offers something rare in middle age: a clear goal, a fixed date, and a definite result.

> "At work, nothing is ever finished, it just gets handed to the next meeting," said a 51-year-old accountant who completed her first marathon in October. "This had a start and an end, and I was the only one who could get me across it."

Training plans have adapted accordingly. Coaches who work with older beginners talk less about speed work and more about recovery days, strength sessions, and sleep. Many of their clients run four days a week rather than six, and spend the rest of the time cycling, swimming, or lifting weights.

## Races are redesigning around them

Organizers are responding in practical ways. Cutoff times on several courses have been extended by thirty minutes or more, keeping roads closed longer so slower finishers are not rushed onto sidewalks. Medical tents have added staff trained to handle heart and heat issues common in older runners.

Other changes are subtler:

- Pacing groups now cover finish times up to six hours instead of stopping at four and a half.
- Age-group awards have been split into five-year bands, so a 47-year-old is not racing a 41-year-old for a podium.
- Expos feature talks on joint health and nutrition alongside the usual shoe stalls.

The money matters too. Older runners tend to have more disposable income, travel farther for races, and bring family members who book hotel rooms and restaurant tables. For host cities, a field heavy with forty- and fifty-somethings is a field that spends.

## The young are not gone, just elsewhere

None of this means younger runners have abandoned the sport. Many have drifted toward shorter races, trail events, and evening run clubs that double as social scenes. The half marathon remains the most popular distance for runners under thirty.

The marathon, though, is finding a second life as a midlife milestone, the way a first triathlon or a long-distance hike once was. At the finish area this fall, the photographs told the story: gray hair, wide grins, foil blankets, and teenage children holding signs for their parents rather than the other way around.

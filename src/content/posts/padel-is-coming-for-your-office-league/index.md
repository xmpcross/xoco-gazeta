It is half past six on a Tuesday, and the glass box on court three is loud. Four colleagues from an accounting firm are locked in a tiebreak, two of them still in office shoes. The ball skims off the back wall, a lob drifts over the net, and someone from payroll smashes it into the fence to win the set. Their manager, who missed the last three shots, is laughing hardest.

Twenty minutes later, another four from the same company take the court. The firm has booked it every Tuesday until spring.

## Why it works for people who are not athletes

Padel looks like tennis played inside a squash court. The court is smaller, the rackets are solid and short, and the walls stay in play. Serves are underarm. Those details make the game far more forgiving than the sports most offices have tried before.

A beginner can rally within a few minutes, which is rare in racket sports. Because it is almost always played as doubles, a strong player and a weak one can share a side without the match falling apart. And because the court is enclosed, the ball rarely goes far, so less time is spent chasing it.

> "In tennis, the new person spends the hour picking up balls. In padel, they are hitting winners by the second game."
>
> — a club coach who runs weekly corporate sessions

## From five-a-side to glass walls

For years, the default office sport was football on a rented pitch or an evening at the bowling alley. Both have limits. Five-a-side tends to attract the same small group of regulars, and bowling is more social than active.

Padel clubs have spotted the gap. Many now sell corporate packages with fixed weekly slots, rental rackets and a coach for the first few sessions. Some run inter-company leagues with simple rules:

- Mixed doubles encouraged, with teams rotated every few weeks
- Short matches, so more people get court time in an evening
- A shared table afterward, which some players admit is the real draw

## The catch is the court sheet

The sport's popularity has its own problem: there are not enough courts. New venues are opening in warehouses, on rooftops and beside existing tennis clubs, but prime evening slots fill weeks in advance. Some clubs have started holding back courts for walk-in players so regulars and corporate bookings do not crowd everyone else out.

Prices vary widely, and indoor courts in particular can be expensive to build and heat. Club owners say demand has held up so far, but they are watching closely to see whether padel follows the path of earlier fitness crazes.

On court three, the accounting team has finished and is packing up. Anna, a junior analyst who joined in the summer, says she had never held a racket before her first Tuesday. She now plays twice a week, once with colleagues and once with friends she met on the court next door. "It is the only meeting I never want to skip," she says.

Outside the chamber doors, there is a new piece of furniture: a wooden cabinet lined with small numbered lockers, each one just big enough for a phone. Members file past it before the morning sitting, drop their devices in, and pocket a key. A clerk keeps an eye on the line. Inside, for the first time in years, nobody is looking down.

A growing number of legislatures have adopted some version of this rule, from local councils to national assemblies. The details vary. Some ban phones entirely during debates, others allow them only for voting apps or emergencies, and a few simply ask members to keep them silent and out of sight. The goal is the same everywhere: get people to listen to each other again.

## The case for the lockbox

Supporters of the bans point to a familiar picture. A member rises to speak, and across the room, a dozen colleagues are scrolling, typing, or filming clips for social media. Debates have drifted into performances aimed at an outside audience, they argue, while the people in the room barely pay attention.

> You'd stand up to make a point you'd worked on for days, and you'd see the tops of forty heads. Now at least they look at you, even if they still disagree.

That was how one long-serving council member described the change after her chamber's rule took effect. She admits she found the first few weeks uncomfortable. Without her phone, she says, she had to sit with the boredom of a slow afternoon, and with the occasional surprise of hearing a good argument from the other side.

## What changed, and what didn't

Early observations from chambers with bans tend to share a few themes:

- Debates feel more attentive, with more direct replies to previous speakers
- Fewer short clips are recorded from the floor for instant posting
- Members spend more time in the corridors, where phones are allowed

That last point is the catch. Some of the activity that left the chamber simply moved next door. Staff report busier lobbies, longer breaks, and members stepping out to check messages before key votes. Clerks in several places have had to adjust schedules to account for the extra traffic.

There are practical frictions too. Members who rely on their phones for notes, translation, or accessibility tools have pushed for exemptions, and most rules now include them. Parents and caregivers have asked how they can be reached in an emergency, which has led some chambers to set up a staffed message desk.

## The harder question

Whether the bans make politics better is difficult to measure. Votes do not seem to change much, and partisan lines remain where they were. What shifts, according to people who work in these buildings, is the texture of the room: fewer interruptions, longer exchanges, and a sense that debate is happening for the people present rather than for a feed.

Critics say that is a cosmetic fix for deeper problems, and that elected officials should be trusted to manage their own attention. Supporters counter that nobody expects a phone ban to fix democracy, only to give it a slightly better room to work in.

At the end of the sitting, the members file back out past the cabinet. The keys turn, the phones come out, and within seconds the corridor fills with the familiar glow of screens.

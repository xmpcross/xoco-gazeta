On a narrow atoll where the highest ground is barely taller than a basketball hoop, a schoolteacher named Mele marks the tide line on a coconut palm near her classroom. Each year the notch creeps a little higher. During the biggest spring tides, seawater bubbles up through the coral sand and pools between the desks.

For small island nations, scenes like that have become the backdrop to an unusual legal strategy. After years of pleading for action at climate summits, a coalition of island governments is asking international courts and tribunals to spell out what large polluting countries owe them. The aim is not a single dramatic verdict but a body of legal opinion that can shape negotiations, lawsuits, and aid for decades.

## From negotiating tables to courtrooms

Climate talks run on consensus, which means the countries with the most to lose often have the least leverage. Courts work differently. A small state with a strong argument can stand on equal footing with a superpower, at least for the length of a hearing.

Island lawyers have built their case on existing law rather than new treaties. They point to long-standing obligations not to cause serious harm to other states, to protect the marine environment, and to respect human rights, including the right of people to live in their own homeland. Their argument is that pouring greenhouse gases into the atmosphere, with the consequences well understood, can breach those duties.

> We are not asking for charity. We are asking the law to describe what is already happening to us.

That is how a legal adviser who has worked on the coalition's filings from the start sums up the strategy. Advisory opinions from international courts are not directly enforceable, but they carry weight. Domestic judges cite them, negotiators quote them, and they make it harder for governments to argue that climate harm is someone else's problem.

## Who owns the sea when the land is gone

Rising water raises a question the law of the sea was never designed to answer. Maritime boundaries, including the exclusive economic zones that give countries rights over fishing and the seabed, are measured from the coastline. If the coast retreats or an island disappears, does the zone shrink with it?

Island states argue that boundaries, once declared, should stay fixed no matter what the sea does. Several regional groups have backed that view, and support is spreading. For a country whose fishing licenses pay for schools and clinics, the stakes are enormous.

Beneath that lies an even harder question: whether a nation can go on existing if its territory becomes uninhabitable. Some governments are already preparing for that possibility by:

- Digitizing land records, archives, and cultural heritage
- Negotiating migration pathways with larger neighbors
- Declaring in law that their statehood continues regardless of physical change

## Patience as a strategy

Legal campaigns move slowly, and island leaders know that no court opinion will stop a single wave. What the cases offer is leverage: a clearer set of rules to cite when asking for adaptation funding, compensation for loss and damage, or faster cuts to emissions.

Back on the atoll, Mele has started teaching her students about the cases. A few of them now say they want to become lawyers. "They understand that the ocean is coming either way," she said. "They just want someone to write down that it wasn't fair."

The building does not look like much from the road: a long gray box, a fenced lot, a row of humming units along one wall. Most residents drive past without a second glance. But at the town council meeting last month, the gray box was the only item anyone wanted to talk about, and the conversation was not about jobs or tax revenue. It was about water.

Data centers have become the quiet backbone of modern life, hosting everything from photo backups to the large AI models that answer questions and write code. They also produce enormous amounts of heat. Getting rid of that heat, in many designs, means evaporating water, and lots of it.

## Where the water goes

The basic problem is simple. Thousands of servers packed into one hall turn electricity into warmth. If that warmth is not removed, the machines slow down or fail. The cheapest way to move heat in many climates is evaporative cooling, which works much like sweat on skin: water absorbs heat as it turns to vapor and drifts away.

A large facility can draw as much water on a hot day as a small town. Much of it comes from the same municipal supply that serves homes and farms. Some operators use treated wastewater or rainwater, but many still rely on drinking-quality water because it is easier on equipment.

> "Nobody told us the new neighbor would be drinking from the same well," said a dairy farmer whose land borders a recently built campus. "I'm not against the internet. I just want to know there's enough to go around in August."

## Cooler designs, at a cost

The industry is not standing still. Newer facilities increasingly use closed-loop systems that recirculate the same water, or air cooling that relies on fans and cold outside temperatures. Some sites pipe liquid directly to the hottest chips, which is more efficient than chilling an entire room.

Each option involves trade-offs:

- Closed-loop and air-cooled systems save water but often use more electricity.
- Building in cold climates helps, but those regions may lack grid capacity or fiber links.
- Reusing wastewater requires treatment plants and pipelines that take years to approve.

Some operators have begun publishing water use for individual sites, though reporting is far from standard. Without consistent numbers, local officials often negotiate permits with little sense of what a facility will actually consume once it is running at full load.

## Towns want a seat at the table

What is changing fastest is the politics. Planning boards that once welcomed data centers as clean, quiet investments now ask harder questions. Some are attaching conditions to permits: caps on water use during droughts, requirements to use recycled supplies, or commitments to fund upgrades to local treatment plants.

Others are exploring ways to make the heat useful. Warm water from cooling systems can feed greenhouses, swimming pools, or district heating networks for nearby homes, turning a waste product into a public benefit.

For the residents who filled that council chamber, the request was modest. They did not want the gray box torn down. They wanted a meter, a public number, and a plan for the next dry summer.

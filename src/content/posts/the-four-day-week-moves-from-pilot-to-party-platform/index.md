On a Friday morning at a mid-sized engineering firm, the open-plan office is almost silent. Chairs are pushed in, monitors are dark, and the only sound is the hum of the ventilation. Two years ago the company joined a trial of a four-day week. It never went back. Staff work four slightly longer days, the office closes on Fridays, and managers say output has held steady.

Scenes like that used to be curiosities written up in business pages. Now they are campaign material. Over the past year, the four-day week has migrated from corporate pilot programs into the platforms of political parties, and the debate has changed character as it moved.

## From experiment to promise

The early trials were voluntary and small. Companies signed up, measured productivity and wellbeing, and reported back. Many found that people were less tired, took fewer sick days, and were less likely to quit, while getting roughly the same amount done. Those results traveled quickly, partly because they offered a rare piece of good news about work.

Politicians noticed. Some parties now promise to pilot a shorter week in the public sector. Others propose tax incentives for private employers that cut hours without cutting pay. A few go further and talk about gradually lowering the standard working week in law.

> Friday used to be the day I did all my errands in a panic after work. Now it's the day I actually see my kids before dinner. I'd vote for whoever keeps that.

That is how one office manager, who has worked a compressed week for 18 months, summed up the politics of it. Pollsters say her view is common among people who have tried the schedule and much less common among those who have not.

## The jobs that don't fit the model

The harder questions arrive when the idea leaves the office. Hospitals, schools, transit systems, and warehouses do not close on Fridays. Cutting hours there means hiring more people, and that costs money that someone has to find.

Critics argue that a policy built on knowledge-work pilots risks creating a two-tier workforce: desk workers with long weekends and frontline staff covering the gaps. Supporters respond that the point is to set a direction, not to flip a switch overnight, and that rotating schedules can spread the benefit more fairly.

Small business owners raise a different concern. A large firm can absorb a trial year. A family-run shop with six employees may not have the slack.

## What a policy would actually look like

The versions on the table differ widely, but most share a few features:

- A gradual phase-in over several years rather than a single deadline
- Public sector trials first, with results published before wider rollout
- Flexibility for employers to choose which day, or to rotate days off
- Protections so that shorter hours do not quietly become lower pay

Whether any of it survives contact with a budget is another matter. Campaign promises are cheap; staffing a hospital ward for a shorter week is not.

Back at the engineering firm, the question feels settled. On Thursday afternoon the office gets a little louder as people wrap up their week. By the next morning, the desks are empty again.

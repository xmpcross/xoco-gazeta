At two in the morning, the volunteer in room four has been asleep for three hours. Wires run from a cap on her head to a screen in the next room, where a sleep technician named Ruth watches slow, rolling waves spread across the display. To an outsider it looks like nothing is happening. Ruth knows better. This is some of the busiest work the brain does all day.

For a long time, sleep was treated as simple rest, a pause between the useful parts of life. Over the past two decades, sleep labs have painted a very different picture: a night shift with several distinct jobs, each one hard to make up later.

## Filing the day away

The first job is memory. During deep sleep, the brain replays patterns from the day, moving fresh experiences from short-term storage into longer-lasting networks. Studies that teach volunteers a new skill in the evening often find they perform better the next morning than they did at the end of practice, as long as they slept in between.

Dreaming sleep seems to do something different. Rather than storing facts, it appears to help link new information to older memories and to soften the emotional edge of difficult experiences. People who are short on this stage tend to react more strongly to stress the next day.

> "Patients tell me they are tired. What they usually mean is that everything feels harder: work, people, even small decisions."
>
> — a sleep technician who has run overnight studies for twelve years

## The overnight cleaning crew

The second job is maintenance. Brain cells produce waste as they work, and during deep sleep the fluid that surrounds them appears to flow more freely, carrying some of that waste away. Researchers are still working out the details, and the picture is far from complete, but the idea has helped explain why chronic poor sleep is linked to so many long-term health concerns.

Short nights also affect the body's chemistry in ways people notice quickly. After even a few days of restricted sleep, many volunteers report:

- Stronger cravings for sugary and salty food
- Slower reaction times, especially late in the afternoon
- More trouble focusing on long or repetitive tasks

## Small habits, steady nights

The encouraging news is that most adults do not need elaborate routines to sleep better. Sleep specialists tend to return to a short, unglamorous list: a consistent wake-up time, morning daylight, a cooler bedroom and fewer screens in the last hour before bed. Caffeine after lunch and alcohol late in the evening are frequent culprits in broken nights.

Back in the lab, the waves on Ruth's screen shift as the volunteer drifts into dreaming sleep, her eyes flicking beneath closed lids. In the morning she will remember nothing of the night except that she slept surprisingly well, wires and all. Ruth will spend the day reading the record of everything her brain did while she was gone.

The checkout screen at a mid-sized home goods chain used to offer a simple choice: card or cash. Now it offers five. Shoppers can split a purchase into four payments, load their change onto a store wallet, earn a higher rewards rate with the chain's own debit card, or open a savings account that pays a bonus when the balance is spent in the store. The cashier, a college student named Theo, says he spends more time explaining the options than ringing up couches.

Retailers have long dabbled in finance, usually through store credit cards issued by a partner bank. What is new is the scale of the ambition. Grocers, apparel brands, delivery apps, and even coffee chains are building payment, lending, and savings products of their own, often through partnerships with smaller banks that handle the regulatory plumbing behind the scenes.

## Why the till is turning into a teller

The logic is straightforward. Every card payment costs a merchant a fee, typically a small percentage of the sale. For a business with thin margins, keeping even part of that fee in-house is meaningful. Store wallets and direct account payments can trim those costs while giving the retailer something even more valuable: a detailed view of how its customers spend.

Financial products also tie people to a store in a way coupons never could. Someone whose paycheck lands in a retailer's account, or who owes a balance on its installment plan, is more likely to come back. Executives describe it as moving from a transaction to a relationship.

> We used to fight for the customer at the shelf. Now we fight for them at the moment they get paid.

That is how a payments manager at a regional retail chain describes the shift, and competitors are drawing the same conclusion.

## The risks hiding in the fine print

Consumer advocates are less enthusiastic. Installment plans make it easy to stack several small debts that add up to a large one, and late fees are not always obvious at the moment of purchase. Store-branded accounts may carry deposit insurance through a partner bank, but customers do not always understand who actually holds their money or what happens if the partnership ends.

There are questions about data, too. A retailer that knows both what you buy and what you earn has an unusually complete picture of your life. Some regulators have begun asking how that information is used to set credit limits or target offers, and whether shoppers have a meaningful way to opt out.

For the retailers themselves, the business carries real risk. Lending means absorbing losses when borrowers fall behind, and a slowdown in consumer spending could turn a profitable side business into a drag on earnings.

## What shoppers can do

Financial counselors offer a short checklist for anyone tempted by a store account:

- Find out which bank actually holds the money before signing up
- Treat installment plans as debt, not as a discount
- Check whether the rewards are worth more than the interest or fees involved

Theo has started using one of the store wallets himself, because the extra discount on employee purchases was too good to pass up. "It's convenient," he said. "I just try not to think about how much they know about my weekends."
